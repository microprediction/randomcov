\begin{abstract}
A claim about the behavior of a covariance or correlation method is a claim
about a distribution over matrices, and the distribution is rarely stated.
Papers validate estimators, factorizations, and portfolio constructions on
whatever generator is at hand: a Wishart draw, a uniform LKJ sample, one
empirical panel. We measure how much this omission can matter. Across
fifteen named ensembles at identical dimension and sample size, the
Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage intensity ranges from $0.08$ to $0.87$; a probability
algorithm whose median error is $10^{-5}$ under twelve ensembles degrades by
two orders of magnitude under kernel-generated fields, for a stated
structural reason. The \texttt{randomcov} library names the measures and
seeds the draws. The program this enables is an audit: re-run published
covariance-method claims across the full set of named ensembles and report
which verdicts survive and which flip. Two demonstrations are given here;
the audits are the invitation.
\end{abstract}

\section{The unstated measure}

Empirical claims about covariance methods have the form ``estimator $A$
beats estimator $B$,'' ``the shrinkage intensity is typically moderate,''
``the factorization recovers structure,'' with performance averaged over
random test matrices. The average is with respect to some generating law,
and the law is usually inherited from convenience: \texttt{numpy} Wishart
draws, a uniform LKJ prior, a single equity panel. Different laws produce
matrices with different spectra, different locality, and different factor
content, so a verdict certified under one law carries no warrant under
another. The omission is not a technicality, as the two measurements below
show.

\section{Fifteen named ensembles}

\texttt{randomcov} generates correlation matrices under named, seeded
measures: \texttt{lkj}, \texttt{onion}, \texttt{vine} (elliptope samplers);
\texttt{wishart}, \texttt{spectrum} (spectral laws, including
Marchenko--Pastur and spiked); \texttt{factor}, \texttt{hierarchical},
\texttt{block\_equicorr} (low-rank and clustered structure);
\texttt{ar1}, \texttt{kernel} (banded and locality-driven fields);
\texttt{sparse\_precision} (graphical-model structure);
\texttt{archakov\_hansen} (log-matrix parameterization);
\texttt{residuals}, \texttt{walk}, \texttt{animals} (empirical-flavored and
trajectory ensembles). Variance vectors are drawn separately, so
correlation structure and scale can be crossed. Every draw is reproducible
from a seed, which is what an audit needs.

\section{Demonstration: one estimator, twelve-fold spread}

The Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage intensity is a workhorse diagnostic: how far the
sample covariance is pulled toward its target. Fix $n=30$ variables, $T=60$
observations, eight replications, and vary only the generating ensemble.

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
ensemble & intensity & sd \\
\midrule
animals & $0.075$ & $0.013$ \\
residuals & $0.095$ & $0.011$ \\
factor & $0.104$ & $0.013$ \\
archakov\_hansen & $0.117$ & $0.015$ \\
kernel & $0.140$ & $0.091$ \\
walk & $0.195$ & $0.034$ \\
block\_equicorr & $0.261$ & $0.097$ \\
vine & $0.344$ & $0.024$ \\
lkj & $0.352$ & $0.026$ \\
hierarchical & $0.357$ & $0.074$ \\
onion & $0.363$ & $0.035$ \\
ar1 & $0.364$ & $0.250$ \\
spectrum & $0.471$ & $0.066$ \\
wishart & $0.528$ & $0.032$ \\
sparse\_precision & $0.869$ & $0.067$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The intensity spans $0.075$ to $0.869$ at identical $(n,T)$: a paper
reporting ``typical'' shrinkage from one generator is reporting a property
of its generator. The ordering is interpretable---strong low-rank structure
(\texttt{animals}, \texttt{residuals}, \texttt{factor}) wants little
shrinkage, near-unstructured draws (\texttt{wishart},
\texttt{sparse\_precision}) want a great deal---and the within-ensemble
standard deviation is itself informative: \texttt{ar1} spans $0.25$ across
its own parameter draws, so even a named ensemble can hide a range.

\section{Demonstration: one algorithm, one failing ensemble}

A deterministic algorithm for multinomial probit choice probabilities
(the \texttt{winning} package; paper in that repository) reports median
absolute error by ensemble after fitting a structured covariance grammar.
Under twelve of the fifteen measures the error is
$1$--$4\times10^{-5}$; under dense-strong ensembles it is
$1$--$4\times10^{-4}$; under \texttt{kernel} fields (Mat\'ern and RBF) it
is $4\times10^{-3}$. The failure has a mechanism: kernel covariance is
locality, approximately Markov, and offers a low-rank factorization no good
purchase. A validation run on any single ensemble would have reported
either ``essentially exact'' or ``inadequate,'' both correctly, for the
same method.

\section{The audit program}

The protocol is short. Take a published claim about a covariance or
correlation method; identify the generating law under which it was
demonstrated; re-run the demonstration, seeded, under every named ensemble;
report the verdict per ensemble; flag the flips and, where possible, the
mechanism. Candidate claims, in rough order of exposure: shrinkage-target
and intensity recommendations; hierarchical-risk-parity versus
minimum-variance horse races; sparse precision recovery guarantees;
condition-number and regularization benchmarks; correlation-forecast
comparisons. The two demonstrations above suggest what to expect:
structure-sensitive methods will flip on structure-heavy ensembles, and
locality is the ensemble property most likely to defeat spectral intuition.

This is a working paper and a program, not a completed audit: the
demonstrations are ours, the audits are the invitation, and the library
exists so that the next paper's claim can be tested across all fifteen
measures in the time it currently takes to test one.
